Two hard drives can be identical on every line of the spec sheet — same
capacity, same 3.5-inch body, same SATA interface, same 5400 RPM — and behave
completely differently inside an array. The difference is how the tracks are
written, and it is the specification manufacturers are least willing to print
on the box.

## What shingling actually does

A conventional drive (**CMR**, conventional magnetic recording) writes each
track in its own lane. The write head is wider than the read head, so the lanes
must be spaced for the write head, and some platter area goes unused.

A shingled drive (**SMR**) overlaps the tracks like roof tiles. The read head
is narrow enough to still pick out each track, so density goes up perhaps 20%
for free.

The cost arrives on writes. Overwriting a shingled track corrupts the tracks
overlapping it, so the drive cannot do it in place: it must read the whole band,
modify, and rewrite it. Drive-managed SMR hides this behind a CMR-mode cache
region and reshuffles later, during idle time.

Sequential writes are fine. Sustained random writes exhaust the cache, and when
they do, throughput does not degrade gracefully — it falls off a cliff, from
roughly 150 MB/s to single-digit megabytes per second, and stays there.

## Why an array is the worst case

A RAID rebuild is precisely the workload SMR handles worst: hours of sustained
writes to every sector of the replacement drive, with no idle time to reorganise
in.

Three things follow.

1. **Rebuilds take days rather than hours.** A 12TB shingled drive can take
   over a week where its CMR equivalent takes about a day.
2. **Some controllers give up.** If writes stall long enough, the controller can
   decide the drive has failed and drop it mid-resilver. On a single-parity
   array that turns a degraded array into a lost one.
3. **The window of exposure is the rebuild.** Every hour a rebuild runs is an
   hour a second failure destroys the array. Multiplying that window by ten is
   the entire risk.

ZFS resilvering is not exempt; neither is Synology SHR, unRAID parity, or a
Btrfs balance. Anything that writes an entire disk is the bad case.

## How to tell which one you are buying

Rarely from the title. The specification is usually stated only in a datasheet
footnote, and drive-managed SMR is by design invisible to the host.

That is why this site resolves recording technology from a **curated family
table** rather than from the listing, and why a drive it cannot resolve is
withheld rather than guessed at.

| Usually CMR                       | Usually SMR                                      |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Seagate IronWolf and IronWolf Pro | Seagate BarraCuda (non-Pro), 2.5-inch especially |
| Seagate Exos, Constellation       | WD Blue in several capacities                    |
| WD Red Plus and Red Pro           | WD My Passport, most 2.5-inch portables          |
| WD Gold, HGST/WD Ultrastar        | Seagate Archive HDD                              |
| Toshiba MG and MN series          | Toshiba P300 in some capacities                  |
| Seagate SkyHawk, WD Purple Pro    | Many low-cost desktop drives, unlabelled         |

Two warnings about that table. **WD Red without "Plus" or "Pro"** is the one
that caught everyone — WD shipped SMR under the NAS brand in 2020 without
saying so, which is where the current level of suspicion comes from. And within
a family the answer can vary by capacity and production run, which is why the
model number matters more than the family name: `WD80EFAX` and `WD80EFBX` are
not the same drive.

## When SMR is genuinely fine

The failure mode is sustained random writes. Where writes are sequential and
bulk, and reads are rare, shingling costs you nothing and the density discount
is real:

- Cold backup and archive targets.
- Write-once media libraries.
- A single external drive you fill and shelve.

For those, [SMR drives](/hdd/smr) are the cheaper honest choice. For anything
with parity, buy [CMR](/hdd/cmr).
