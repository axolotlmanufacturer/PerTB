Every other component in a PC advertises the number you actually care about. A
CPU tells you cores and clocks; RAM tells you capacity and speed. Storage tells
you a price and a capacity and leaves the division to you — and because the
capacities span two orders of magnitude, the sticker price carries almost no
information. A 2TB NVMe SSD and a 20TB hard drive routinely cost the same money.

## The arithmetic

```
price per TB = (item price + shipping) ÷ (capacity of one drive × number of drives)
```

Three details in that line do all the work.

### Terabytes are decimal, not binary

A drive sold as "20TB" contains 20 × 10¹² bytes. Formatted, your operating
system will report roughly 18.2 TiB, because it divides by 2⁴⁰ instead of 10¹².
Nothing has been lost and nothing is being hidden from you — they are two
different units with confusingly similar names.

We price against the advertised figure, because that is the figure you are
comparing across listings. A site that quoted TiB would show numbers about 10%
higher than every competitor for identical drives, and every visitor would
correctly conclude the numbers were wrong.

### Shipping is part of the price

It is included here by default, and turning it off is a deliberate act.

Leave shipping out and the top of any table that includes eBay fills up with
$0.99 items carrying $28 postage. That is not a pricing quirk to be normalised
away; it is a listing strategy, and a comparison that ignores it ranks the
listings that use it first, permanently.

Some eBay listings report **calculated** shipping, where the cost depends on
your postcode and is not knowable until you enter it. Those are marked
`shipping unknown` and counted as zero. That understates them. It is the only
honest option — the alternative is to invent a figure.

### A lot of five is not one drive

`Lot of 5 × 4TB, $260` is 20TB for $260, which is $13/TB. Read as a single 4TB
drive it comes out at $65/TB — or, far worse, read the other way round, a
listing priced per-drive gets divided by the lot total and lands at the top of
the table at a price nobody can buy.

Every row here shows a `lot of N` badge where the listing is a multi-drive lot,
and the **Hide multi-drive lots** toggle removes them entirely. If you are
filling one bay, use it.

## What $/TB does not tell you

It is a single scalar, and drives differ along axes it cannot see.

- **Endurance.** A QLC SSD and a TLC SSD at the same $/TB are not the same
  purchase if you write a lot. Check the TBW rating.
- **Sustained write speed.** Consumer SSDs quote a burst figure served by an
  SLC cache. Fill the cache on a QLC drive and the floor can be slower than a
  hard drive.
- **Recording technology.** See the [CMR vs SMR
  guide](/guides/cmr-vs-smr-and-raid) — for an array this is the difference
  between a rebuild that finishes and one that does not.
- **Remaining life on used drives.** A datacentre pull at $4/TB with 45,000
  power-on hours and no returns is a different proposition from the same price
  at 8,000 hours with a 30-day window.
- **Warranty.** Shucking an external voids it. Enterprise pulls usually have
  none left at all.

$/TB is the right first cut, and it is the number nobody publishes. It is not
the whole decision.

## Three ways the figure gets quoted wrong

1. **Against MSRP rather than the live price.** A "62% off" $/TB computed from
   a list price nobody has charged in two years.
2. **Excluding shipping, silently.** Common, and it inverts the ranking at the
   cheap end.
3. **On a lot, without saying so.** The single most expensive mistake in this
   whole category, and the reason lot detection is the most-tested rule in this
   codebase.
