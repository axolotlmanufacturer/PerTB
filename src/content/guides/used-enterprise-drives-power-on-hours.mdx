The lowest genuine $/TB on the market is not a sale. It is a datacentre
decommissioning its three-year-old fleet, and the drives arriving on eBay by the
pallet. Enterprise helium drives that cost several hundred dollars new turn up
at a quarter of the price with five figures on the hour counter.

That is a real trade, and it is worth making with the numbers in front of you.

## What power-on hours actually mean

Power-on hours count the time the drive has been spinning. Not reads, not
writes, not thermal cycles — just hours energised.

For context: **one year of continuous operation is 8,760 hours.** So a drive
advertised at 45,000 hours has run for roughly five years, which is a normal
service life for a datacentre and past the warranty of almost any consumer
drive.

Two things temper the alarm.

**Enterprise drives are specified for it.** A drive rated at 2.5 million hours
MTBF with a 550 TB/year workload rating is built to spin continuously for five
years. That is what you are buying.

**Constant operation is the gentle case.** The most-cited large-scale failure
data — Backblaze publishes theirs quarterly — shows failure concentrated at the
very start of life and again several years in, with a long flat middle. Drives
that spin up and down repeatedly, or run hot, or get knocked, do worse than
drives that spin steadily in a cool rack.

A five-year-old drive that has spent those years in a temperature-controlled
datacentre is often in better condition than a three-year-old drive that has
lived under someone's desk.

## The numbers that should worry you more

If a seller will give you a SMART report, these matter more than the hour count:

- **Reallocated sector count (SMART 5).** Sectors that failed and were remapped
  to spares. Zero is what you want. Anything non-zero and rising is a drive
  actively failing.
- **Current pending sector count (SMART 197).** Sectors that failed a read and
  are awaiting reallocation. Worse than reallocated: the data is at risk now.
- **Uncorrectable errors (SMART 198).** Non-zero means reads have already
  failed outright.
- **Start/stop and load-unload cycles.** A drive with 12,000 hours and 40,000
  load cycles has had a harder life than one with 45,000 hours and 200.
- **Helium level (SMART 22)**, on sealed helium drives. Below 100 is a leak,
  and a leaking helium drive is finished.

A listing offering zero reallocated sectors at 45,000 hours is a better buy than
one offering 200 reallocated sectors at 9,000.

## What "tested" means, and does not

"Tested" in a listing title usually means the seller ran a short SMART self-test
and confirmed the drive spins up and reports PASSED. That is a check for
already-dead, not a prediction.

Terms worth distinguishing:

- **Manufacturer recertified** — returned to the manufacturer, tested, and
  reissued with fresh warranty, often 1–2 years. The safest used tier and priced
  accordingly.
- **Refurbished** — anyone's word for anything. Ask who did it.
- **Pull / tested pull** — removed from working equipment, powered on,
  confirmed alive.
- **As-is** — no claim at all.

## Working the risk into the price

Buy in numbers and treat failure as a running cost rather than an event.

- **Assume some arrive dead.** Budget a spare. A 5% DOA rate at a third of the
  price is still far ahead.
- **Test before you trust.** Run a full surface read — `badblocks -svn` or a
  long SMART self-test — before any drive holds data you care about. Most
  failures that are going to happen early happen in the first fortnight.
- **Parity, not hope.** These belong in an array with redundancy you would
  accept losing a member of, and they belong under a real backup.
- **Check the return window.** Thirty days of returns is worth real money per
  terabyte, because it covers exactly the period when early failures show up.
  Every used row here shows the seller's return policy for that reason.
